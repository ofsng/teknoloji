# Heuristic Search and Intelligent Problem Solving: Classical Foundations, Learning-Augmented Algorithms and Applications

## 1. Araştırmanın özeti

> Heuristic search has been a central paradigm of artificial intelligence since the earliest work on mechanised problem solving, and it remains embedded in automated planning, robot navigation, combinatorial optimisation, game playing and, increasingly, machine-learning systems that couple learned models with explicit search. Its literature, however, is divided among communities that rarely evaluate one another’s claims: optimal and bounded-suboptimal state-space search, constraint and satisfiability solving, local search and metaheuristics, adversarial and sampling-based search, and learning-based and language-model-based problem solving. This critical narrative review examines how heuristic knowledge is obtained, what guarantees it supports, how its performance is evidenced and where modern learning-based methods alter or leave intact the classical picture. Literature was identified through structured searches of multidisciplinary and discipline-specific scholarly indexes, supplemented by backward and forward citation tracing, with peer-reviewed journal articles and archival proceedings appraised for methodological adequacy, strength of baselines and transparency of computational reporting. The synthesis indicates that formal results on admissibility, consistency and node expansion remain the most secure part of the field, but their practical value depends on assumptions that learned and inadmissible heuristics frequently violate. Automatically derived heuristics from relaxation and abstraction are well supported in planning benchmarks, whereas learned heuristics show strong single-domain results and weaker, less consistent generalisation. Metaheuristic research combines genuinely effective problem-specific methods with a large volume of metaphor-driven proposals whose novelty and benchmarking practices have been seriously questioned. The most convincing recent advances integrate learned guidance with search and with external verification rather than replacing search altogether, while large language models appear more reliable as generators of candidate heuristics or code than as stand-alone planners. Unresolved questions concern generalisation across instance distributions, fair accounting of computational cost, robustness of learned guidance and reproducible evaluation. Progress is likely to depend less on new algorithmic metaphors than on shared benchmarks, stronger baselines and explicit reporting of what each method guarantees.
